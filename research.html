How to read the milestones

The CDC and AAP revised their checklists in 2022 so each milestone is something at least 75% of children do by that age, not the average age. Missing one is a reason to ask your health visitor, not a diagnosis. Evidence tags below show how much each idea rests on: Strong meta-analyses or several trials, Moderate consistent observational or lab studies, Guidance professional consensus.

When to check in with a professional

By 15 months, ask if he isn't

  • taking a few steps on his own
  • pointing to ask for things
  • saying 1 to 2 words besides mama/dada
  • looking at familiar things when you name them

By 18 months, ask if he

  • isn't walking independently
  • has no words, or doesn't seem to understand simple words
  • doesn't point to show you things
  • doesn't follow one-step instructions without a gesture
  • shows stiffness, floppiness, or one side moving differently

At any age

Act promptly if Arlo loses skills he had, doesn't respond to sounds or his name, or you have a gut feeling something isn't right. Contact your health visitor or GP; you don't need to wait for a review. For suspected poisoning call NHS 111, or 999 in an emergency.

About autism screening

The M-CHAT-R/F is a free parent questionnaire for 16 to 30 months. In its validation study of 16,071 toddlers, about 47.5% of children who still screened positive after the follow-up interview were later diagnosed with autism, and 94.6% had some developmental delay or concern. A positive screen means “look more closely”, not a diagnosis. The UK doesn't screen every child at 18 months, so take any result to your health visitor or GP. Robins et al., Pediatrics, 2014

Arlo's UK review timeline

9–12 months

1-year review with the health visiting team. The ASQ-3 is often used.

13–24 months

No routine review in most areas. Keep a dated log of words, gestures and first steps, and get in touch any time you're concerned.

Sep 2027 – Mar 2028

2 to 2½ year review. In England it uses the ASQ-3 and ASQ:SE-2. If your invitation doesn't arrive, contact your health visiting team; national data show not every eligible child gets one.

Sleep, screens and safety

Daily basics

  • Sleep: 11 to 14 hours in 24, naps included, with regular times.
  • Movement: at least 180 minutes of varied activity a day; no more than an hour at a time strapped in.
  • Screens: none for 1-year-olds apart from video calls. Read or tell stories together instead.

WHO, 2019

Newly mobile toddler

  • Quarter grapes and cherry tomatoes lengthways; no whole nuts or popcorn.
  • Stair gates top and bottom; anchor drawers and bookcases; window restrictors.
  • No sit-in baby walkers or jumpers.
  • Medicines, cleaning products and laundry capsules locked away; hot drinks out of reach.

NHS; Child Accident Prevention Trust

A note on “boys vs girls”

The largest study pooled parent reports for 13,783 children across 10 European language communities and found girls slightly ahead on average in early gestures, words and word combinations. The difference is small and the groups overlap heavily. CDC, NHS and ASQ-3 expectations aren't sex-specific, and “boys talk later” is never a reason to wait. Eriksson et al., British Journal of Developmental Psychology, 2012

🧭

Honest caveats

  • Milestones describe populations, not individuals. Healthy children vary widely.
  • Strong trial evidence exists mainly for parent coaching in responsive talk and for interactive book-sharing, and those trials often involved children with or at risk of language delay. Effects in typical toddlers may be smaller.
  • Fine-motor ideas like shape sorters and scribbling are sensible practice, not proven accelerators.
  • Romeo (2018) studied 4 to 6-year-olds; Meltzoff (1988) and Sorce (1985) are small lab studies; the walker findings on development are observational, though the injury data are robust; the word-spurt study followed 38 children.
  • The CDC checklists are American. Use UK services: health visitor, GP, NHS 111 and local speech and language therapy self-referral where offered.
  • This page is educational and isn't a substitute for advice from Arlo's own clinicians.
